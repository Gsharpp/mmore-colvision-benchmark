\begin{abstract}
We present an open-source benchmark suite that compares five ColVision
multi-vector retrieval models (ColPali, ColQwen2, ColQwen2.5, ColQwen3 and a
ColGemma3 variant) within a single, fixed RAG pipeline (\textsc{mmore} PR
\#305). Unlike per-model evaluations on \textsc{ViDoRe}, we hold the index,
the reranker, the embedding loader and the retrieval contract constant, and
vary only the visual encoder. Two studies are reported: (A)~an
\emph{English-only scaling study} on a figure-rich PubMed Central Open Access
subset at $\{100, 1k, 10k, 50k\}$ pages, and (B)~a \emph{multilingual study}
on six languages (English, French, German, Spanish, Simplified Chinese,
Arabic) at a fixed corpus size, including one logographic and one RTL
script. Cells are run with three random seeds and reported with 95\%
bootstrap confidence intervals; pairwise comparisons use Wilcoxon signed-rank
with Holm correction. The evaluation proceeds in \emph{passes} of increasing
complexity (Section~\ref{sec:stages}): a small-scale pilot, a cross-lingual
extension, LLM-grounded query sets, and full scaling. Queries are generated by an inverse prompt that asks
\textsc{Meditron}-70B to author the questions a page would uniquely answer; we
validate this methodology by correlating its yield against an annotated
healthcare subset of \textsc{ViDoRe}. We release the benchmark code, the
corpus manifest with per-PDF SHA-256, and the synthetic query sets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Vision-language retrieval over document collections has shifted, in less than
two years, from a research curiosity to a production primitive. The ColVision
line of work --- starting with ColPali~\cite{faysse2024colpali} and continued
by ColQwen2, ColQwen2.5, ColQwen3 and ColGemma3 variants --- has demonstrated
that multi-vector page-level embeddings, combined with late-interaction
reranking inherited from ColBERT~\cite{khattab2020colbert}, can outperform
text-only OCR$\rightarrow$BM25 pipelines on figure-rich corpora. Yet the
public evaluation surface for these encoders is fragmented. Each model is
released with its own \textsc{ViDoRe}~\cite{faysse2024vidore} scores, computed
on the authors' own software stack, often against subtly different versions
of the benchmark. \textsc{MMEB}~\cite{wei2025mmeb} reduces some of this
variance for general multimodal embedding tasks, but does not target document
retrieval and assumes a flat single-vector contract incompatible with
late-interaction encoders.

The result is that practitioners considering ColVision for a real-world
retrieval-augmented-generation (RAG) deployment cannot read off, from
published numbers, which encoder best suits their corpus and constraints.
Three questions are answered nowhere in the public literature:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Cross-model parity.} How do the five ColVision models compare
    when the index, the reranker, the embedding loader and the retrieval
    contract are all held strictly constant?
  \item \textbf{Scaling.} How does each model's retrieval quality, latency
    and memory degrade as the index grows from a few hundred to tens of
    thousands of pages --- the regime an actual hospital or pharmaceutical
    knowledge base operates in?
  \item \textbf{Multilingual robustness.} How much do the models generalise
    across scripts unrelated to their pre-training distribution, including
    logographic (Simplified Chinese) and RTL connected scripts (Arabic)?
\end{enumerate}

We address all three by building a fixed-pipeline benchmark on top of the
\textsc{mmore} project~\cite{mmore2024} (PR \#305), which integrates the five
encoders behind a single CLI. We invoke the \textsc{mmore} CLI as a
subprocess rather than re-implementing its pipeline: every measurement we
report reflects the exact code path a real \textsc{mmore} user would
exercise. The benchmark code, configuration manifests with per-PDF SHA-256,
synthetic query sets and result records (in an
\textsc{MTEB}-compatible~\cite{muennighoff2023mteb} JSON schema) are
released alongside this report.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Visual document retrieval.} ColPali~\cite{faysse2024colpali}
introduced page-level multi-vector retrieval over rendered PDF images, using
PaliGemma as the visual encoder and a ColBERT-style late-interaction scorer.
Subsequent ColQwen2/2.5/3 variants swap the encoder for the Qwen-VL
series~\cite{bai2023qwenvl}, generally trading throughput for quality.
ColGemma3 variants (e.g.~\textit{ColNetraEmbed}) target multilingual
performance through the Gemma-3 backbone~\cite{team2024gemma3}. All four
families are evaluated by their authors on
\textsc{ViDoRe}~\cite{faysse2024vidore} and its v2
extension~\cite{faysse2025vidorev2}, but typically with different software
stacks, different reranker implementations, and different batch-size /
precision configurations, making cross-paper comparison unreliable.

\paragraph{Multimodal embedding benchmarks.}
\textsc{MMEB}~\cite{wei2025mmeb} standardises the multimodal embedding
evaluation surface across 36 datasets, but operates on single-vector
embeddings and on tasks (classification, VQA, grounding) that do not require
page-level multi-vector retrieval. \textsc{MTEB}~\cite{muennighoff2023mteb}
plays the same role for text and provides the result-format conventions we
adopt for this work; ColVision models fall outside its scope.

\paragraph{RAG generation metrics.}
\textsc{RAGAS}~\cite{es2023ragas} proposes LLM-as-judge metrics for
retrieval-augmented generation --- faithfulness, answer relevancy, context
precision and context recall --- which we adopt unchanged. We deliberately
use a single judge (Meditron-70B~\cite{chen2023meditron}) and call out the
inter-judge-agreement gap as a limitation rather than averaging across judges.

\paragraph{Synthetic query generation.} The idea of asking a strong LLM to
author the queries a page would uniquely answer dates back to the
\textsc{InPars}~\cite{bonifacio2022inpars} and \textsc{Promptagator}
~\cite{dai2022promptagator} lines of work in text retrieval. We follow the
same principle in the document-image setting, prompt Meditron-70B with the
page's extracted text plus visual hints, and add a second-pass ambiguity
filter modelled on the same judge. The methodology is validated (Phase 4)
against an annotated subset of \textsc{ViDoRe} healthcare.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Pipeline under test}

\textsc{mmore} PR \#305 wires the ColVision encoders into a single ingest /
retrieve pipeline. Each PDF page is rendered to a 200-dpi PNG via
\textsc{PyMuPDF}~\cite{pymupdf}, then encoded by the chosen ColVision model
into a multi-vector (token-level) embedding tensor. Embeddings are
serialised to Parquet and inserted into a Milvus~\cite{wang2021milvus}
collection (\textsc{FLAT} index, inner-product metric) keyed by the page
identifier. Retrieval issues an embedding-only top-$k$ over Milvus, then
re-ranks the candidates with a ColBERT-style MaxSim score~\cite{khattab2020colbert}
computed in a CPU thread pool. The resulting ranked list is returned to the
caller through a LangChain \textit{Retriever} contract; the generation step,
when used, feeds the top-$k$ pages to a separate generator LLM.

We hold every component of this pipeline constant across models. The only
variables are (a)~the ColVision encoder, (b)~the corpus, and (c)~the random
seed. The Milvus collection name is derived from the model identifier so
that different embedding dimensions do not collide.

\subsection{Models}

The five encoders under evaluation are listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}.

ColPali/ColQwen2/ColQwen2.5/ColGemma3 lines, 320 for ColQwen3), which the
pipeline absorbs by giving each model its own Milvus collection and
injecting the per-model dimension into the index and retrieval configs. The
batch sizes and precision are taken from each model's published

team has released through ColQwen2.5), and the community ports we tested fail
to load cleanly. Its integration is tracked through the staged protocol
(Section~\ref{sec:stages}, Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\subsection{Corpora}
\label{sec:corpora}

\paragraph{Track A (English scaling).} We assemble a figure-rich English
medical corpus from PubMed Central Open Access~\cite{pmcoa}. Articles are
filtered by visual density: for each PDF we compute the fraction of total
page area occupied by figures and tables (\textsc{PyMuPDF} bounding boxes),
and keep PDFs whose density is at least 30\%. The retained PDFs are sampled,
stratified by their MeSH tag, to four palier sizes: 100, 1{,}000, 10{,}000
and 50{,}000 pages. Each palier is a strict subset of the next, so a model
indexing the 50k-page palier has already paid the indexing cost for the
smaller ones --- this is the regime of an organisation that grows its
knowledge base incrementally.

\paragraph{Track B (multilingual).} For each of six languages --- English,
French, German, Spanish, Simplified Chinese, Arabic --- we target
$\approx$1{,}500 pages from a language-appropriate open-access medical
source: PMC-OA for English, HAL+Cairn for French, Thieme OA for German,
SciELO for Spanish, CNKI OA for Chinese, the Saudi Medical Journal for
Arabic. The same visual-density filter is applied. Chinese and Arabic are
selected deliberately to stress the encoders along two orthogonal axes: a
logographic script that shares no characters with the encoders' Latin-heavy
pre-training distribution, and an RTL connected script that breaks the
character-segmentation assumptions baked into most OCR-based pre-training
proxies.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Every corpus is shipped as a
\texttt{CorpusManifest} JSON: for each PDF the manifest records its
SHA-256 hash, page count, detected language, visual density, MeSH tags and
source. The \texttt{bcv-corpus verify} command re-hashes the local files to
detect drift; the manifest hash is recorded in every benchmark result so
that any reported number can be traced back to the bytes that produced it.

\subsection{Synthetic query generation}

We generate one query set per (track, language, palier) tuple. For each
page that passes the visual-density filter, we extract the page text with
PyMuPDF, build a prompt that includes the text plus a structured visual hint
(``this page contains $\geq$1 figure / table''), and ask Meditron-70B (via
vLLM, OpenAI-compatible endpoint) to author exactly two clinical questions
that can be answered \emph{only} by consulting that specific page. The
prompt asks the LLM to flag each question with a \texttt{requires\_visual}
boolean; we keep only those flagged \texttt{true}, ensuring the benchmark
exercises the visual dimension rather than collapsing to a text-retrieval
proxy.

A second pass --- the \emph{ambiguity filter} --- re-presents each
candidate question to the same judge along with the page text, and asks for
a single confidence score in $[0, 1]$ representing whether the question is
(a)~precise enough to admit a single best page-source, and (b)~genuinely
tied to that particular page. Queries below a 0.8 confidence threshold are
dropped. The kept queries are serialised as a \texttt{QuerySet} JSONL whose
SHA-256 is recorded in every downstream result. Volumes are sized to
$\sim$2{,}000 retained queries on Track A (spread across paliers) and
$\sim$200 per language on Track B.

\subsection{Methodology validation}
\label{sec:methodology-validation}

Synthetic queries inherit the biases of the LLM that authored them, in
particular a tendency to over-rely on textual cues that happen to be unique
to a page. We bound this bias by validating the methodology against an
annotated reference: we take a $\sim$50-document healthcare slice of
\textsc{ViDoRe} v2 for which human-authored queries and gold pages exist,
re-generate queries on the same documents with our pipeline, and compute
nDCG@5 on both query sets under an identical retrieval configuration. We
report the Pearson and Spearman correlations between the two per-document
nDCG@5 vectors; the benchmark gates on a Pearson coefficient
$\geq$ 0.85. If the gate fails we iterate on the generation prompt before
running any benchmark cell, on the assumption that a methodology that does
not correlate with human judgement on a known-good subset cannot be trusted
on novel corpora.

\subsection{Staged evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:stages}

We execute the benchmark as a sequence of \emph{passes} of increasing
complexity and realism rather than as a single monolithic run. Each pass is
self-contained --- it produces released artefacts (benchmark records, corpus
manifests, query sets) --- and validates the machinery the next pass depends
on. This design yields an early, trustworthy signal on a small corpus before
committing compute to larger, multilingual and LLM-grounded configurations,
and it makes the marginal contribution of each added dimension (language,
query realism, scale) individually attributable.

\textbf{Pass~1 (pilot)} establishes the pipeline end-to-end on the tiny
English palier and yields the first footprint, latency and retrieval-quality
measurements, using a deterministic template query generator.

logographic (CJK) script --- to probe cross-script robustness.
\textbf{Pass~3 (grounded queries)} replaces template queries with clinical
questions authored by \textsc{Meditron} (validated on the 7B model, finalised
on the 70B) and ambiguity-filtered, producing defensible retrieval-quality
numbers and re-scoring the earlier passes.
\textbf{Pass~4 (scale \& seeds)} grows the English corpus to $\approx$500
documents and runs three random seeds for the statistical analysis
(bootstrap CIs, Wilcoxon/Holm). This release reports Pass~1 in full and
specifies Passes~2--4, whose code, configuration and corpus manifests are
implemented and tested.

\section{Track A --- scaling study}
\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Full benchmark design.}
For each of the five models and four paliers ($\{$100, 1\,000, 10\,000,
50\,000$\}$ pages) we target three random seeds. A cell consists of:
(1)~processing the palier PDFs with \texttt{mmore colpali process},
(2)~indexing the embeddings with \texttt{mmore colpali index},
(3)~running batched retrieval with \texttt{mmore colpali retrieve} over the
palier's query set. Each step's duration, GPU memory peak (via
\textsc{NVML}) and per-query latency are captured in a
\texttt{BenchmarkRecord}. Sixty cells
(5~models~$\times$~4~paliers~$\times$~3~seeds) are the full run target.
Hardware: one H100-NVL GPU per cell, 8 CPU cores, 64~GB RAM (EPFL LiGHT
cluster, Run:AI scheduler).

\paragraph{Pass~1 scope.}
\label{sec:pass1}
The first pass of the protocol (Section~\ref{sec:stages}) covers the
\emph{tiny} palier (90~PDFs, 709~pages after visual-density filtering) at
seed~0, for four of the five models. ColQwen3 is not included: no official
checkpoint exists and the community port fails to load
(Section~\ref{sec:future-work}). Queries for this pass use the template
generator described below rather than Meditron-70B; LLM-grounded queries are
introduced in Pass~3, and larger paliers and additional seeds in Pass~4.

\paragraph{Pass~1 query generation (template-based).}
\label{sec:mock-queries}
For Pass~1 we generate queries from page text using a deterministic template,
deferring LLM-grounded generation to Pass~3. For each page that passes the visual-density filter
we extract the $k=3$ most distinctive content terms (longest alphabetic
tokens, stoplist-filtered), and form an English question whose phrasing is
steered toward the page's visual payload, e.g.\
\textit{``Which page presents a table with data on $t_1$, $t_2$, $t_3$?''}
(or \textit{``\ldots{}shows a figure illustrating\ldots''} when the page
carries figures rather than tables). The English phrasing matches the
English corpus the encoders index, and the visual steering keeps the task on
the multimodal pathway rather than collapsing to plain text matching.
The ground-truth answer is the source page itself, so retrieval metrics
(nDCG@10, MRR) remain well-defined. Only pages with at least one figure or
table (\texttt{has\_figures} or \texttt{has\_tables} flag from PyMuPDF) are
included in the query set. This is an explicitly preliminary methodology with
two known weaknesses: the phrasing is simpler than a clinician-authored
question, and the shared template prefix (\textit{``Which page\ldots''}) is
identical across queries, which can homogenise the query embeddings and bias
an encoder toward globally figure-/table-dense pages rather than the specific
source page. Results should be interpreted as a \emph{system integration
smoke test} rather than a full benchmark.

Table~\ref{tab:track_a_retrieval} reports nDCG@5, nDCG@10, MRR and

stand out, both to be read as preliminary. First, the ColQwen2 family

the encoder returns nearly the same globally figure-dense pages regardless of
the query terms --- consistent with the template-homogenisation weakness

encoder limitation or a model-specific sensitivity to the query phrasing
cannot be resolved without higher-quality queries; we flag this as the first
item of future work. For reference, on \textsc{ViDoRe-v2} the published
ordering at full corpus size places ColQwen2.5 and ColQwen3 ahead of
ColPali-v1.3 and ColQwen2~\cite{faysse2024vidore}; our preliminary tiny-palier
ordering should not be compared directly to it.

\subsection{Generation results}

The RAG generation step (feeding retrieved pages to a generator LLM and
scoring with RAGAS faithfulness and answer relevancy) requires a running
vLLM endpoint serving Meditron-70B~\cite{chen2023meditron}, which the protocol
introduces in Pass~3 (Section~\ref{sec:stages}). Generation results are
reported together with the LLM-grounded query sets from that pass.

The clearest signal is a \textbf{retrieval-latency--index-size trade-off}.
The four encoders emit very different numbers of per-page vectors --- from
241 (ColGemma3) to 1031 (ColPali), a $4.3\times$ spread --- which propagates
directly to the on-disk index (140\,MB vs.\ 600\,MB) and, decisively, to
query latency: median per-query latency ranges from 1.6\,s (ColGemma3) to
12.8\,s (ColPali), an $8\times$ gap. This is mechanistic rather than
incidental: \textsc{mmore}'s MaxSim reranker scores every candidate page by a
dense query--page late-interaction whose cost scales with the number of page
vectors, so a more compact encoder is faster at query time. ColGemma3 is the
most compact and lowest-latency at retrieval, while paying the highest
\emph{ingestion} cost (0.84\,pg/s); ColPali is the opposite. This inverts the
pre-registered ``memory cliff'' hypothesis for ColGemma3
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}): in our configuration ColGemma3 produces
128-dimensional embeddings and the smallest footprint of the four, not the
largest. Per-cell \texttt{BenchmarkRecord} JSON artefacts (with full
p50/p95/p99 latency, GPU peak and timing fields) are released alongside this
report.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

With a single seed the standard deviation over seeds cannot be estimated,
and pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (the planned inference procedure)
are not applicable. We report point estimates only. The planned three-seed,
bootstrap-CI, Holm-corrected analysis is a direct follow-up as soon as
compute permits.

\section{Track B --- multilingual study}

\paragraph{Scope note.}
Track~B is Pass~2 of the protocol (Section~\ref{sec:stages}). The executed
configuration targets three languages chosen for script diversity --- English,
French, and Simplified Chinese (one logographic script) --- each drawn from
real medical-literature sources. The full multilingual design
(5~models~$\times$~6~languages~$\times$~3~seeds, 90 cells) is described below;
the code, configuration and corpus manifests are implemented and tested.
Per-language results are added as the pass completes.

\subsection{Setup}

For each model and language we target three seeds at a fixed
$\approx$1{,}500-page corpus size using the sources listed in
Section~\ref{sec:corpora}. Pass~2 executes the English (PMC-OA), French
(HAL) and Simplified-Chinese subsets; German (Thieme OA), Spanish (SciELO)
and Arabic (Saudi Medical Journal) complete the full design in a later pass.
The instrumentation is identical to Track A, and the English cell uses a
corpus sample disjoint from Track A.

\subsection{Discussion of script effects (CJK, RTL)}

Track B not yet executed. We expect that models pre-trained predominantly

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central design decision in this benchmark is to hold the pipeline
constant and vary only the visual encoder. This is a deliberate departure
from per-model evaluations that each tune their own retrieval stack: it
measures the \emph{encoder's contribution} to end-to-end RAG quality under
deployment-realistic conditions, not the peak performance achievable with
a hand-crafted retriever.

At the tiny palier, the corpus (figure-rich medical PDFs from PMC-OA,
visual density $\geq$30\%) exercises the visual dimension of each encoder:
pages containing at least one figure or table are the only sources of
queries, so a model that relies purely on OCR-extracted text is penalised.
The template query generation used in Pass~1 trades linguistic variety
for determinism; results are thus conservative --- a model that ranks well
under template queries can be expected to rank at least as well under
LLM-authored clinical questions, which typically pick up the same
distinctive domain terms.

We anticipate three effects to emerge more clearly at larger paliers:
(1)~a \emph{diversity wall} where text-biased encoders degrade on
ambiguous queries whose answer depends on figure layout rather than
caption text;
(2)~a \emph{retrieval-latency cliff} driven by the per-page vector count.
Our tiny-palier measurements (Table~\ref{tab:track_a_perf}) already show this
effect: all four executed encoders use 128-dimensional vectors, but they
differ $4.3\times$ in vectors-per-page, and ColPali --- the densest at
$\approx$1031 vectors/page --- is $8\times$ slower per query than the most
compact encoder, ColGemma3. We had pre-registered the opposite hypothesis (a
memory cliff for ColGemma3 on the assumption of 768-d embeddings); the data
contradict it, and we expect the per-vector reranking cost, not the embedding
dimension, to be the dominant scaling bottleneck at the 10k- and 50k-page
paliers;
(3)~a \emph{script-sensitivity gap} in Track~B, expected to be largest for
ColPali (trained predominantly on English document images).

These hypotheses will be tested in the full benchmark run. The current
release establishes the pipeline, the tooling, and a reproducible
corpus-query provenance chain that makes the full run straightforward to
execute and verify.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Template queries, not LLM- or clinician-authored.} The
    retrieval-quality numbers in this release use the deterministic template
    of Section~\ref{sec:mock-queries}, not the planned Meditron-70B
    generation. The shared template prefix can homogenise query embeddings,
    so the absolute scores are a smoke-test signal, not a ranking. LLM- and,
    ultimately, clinician-authored queries are the primary follow-up.
  \item \textbf{Single seed, small query set.} Results cover one seed and
    32~queries on the tiny palier. No standard deviation over seeds can be
    estimated and the pairwise differences in Table~\ref{tab:track_a_retrieval}
    are within sampling noise. The planned three-seed, bootstrap-CI,
    Holm-corrected analysis is deferred.
  \item \textbf{Two encoders at zero.} ColPali~v1.3 and ColGemma3 score zero
    on every retrieval metric under these template queries. We have not
    established whether this is a genuine encoder limitation or a sensitivity
    to the homogeneous query phrasing; it must be re-checked with
    higher-quality queries before any conclusion is drawn.
  \item \textbf{One encoder unmeasured.} ColQwen3 is absent: its published
    HuggingFace identifier does not resolve and the nearest checkpoint fails
    to load under the pinned \texttt{colpali-engine}. A library upgrade is
    required (Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).
  \item \textbf{Single LLM judge (full design).} Both query filtering and
    RAGAS metrics use a single Meditron-70B judge. Inter-judge agreement is
    not measured here; this is an explicit future-work item, not a quality
    claim.
  \item \textbf{Corpus skew.} PMC-OA is US-centric and EN-dominant; the six
    Track~B sources differ in domain mix, image quality and license terms.
    We control for visual density but cannot equalise editorial style.
  \item \textbf{Single GPU class.} All cells run on one 80\,GB-class
    data-center GPU under Run:AI placement (the cluster is heterogeneous, so
    the exact GPU model may vary across cells; latency figures are
    indicative). We do not measure behaviour under tighter VRAM constraints
    (A100~40GB, T4) or mixed-precision fallbacks.
  \item \textbf{One pipeline.} The benchmark holds the pipeline constant
    \emph{by design}, but the absolute numbers we report are conditional on
    \textsc{mmore}'s specific implementation choices (Milvus \textsc{FLAT}
    index, CPU MaxSim reranker, top-$k$=10 default). A different RAG stack
    may reorder the models.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The current release validates the pipeline end-to-end and produces robust
performance measurements; the retrieval-quality numbers are preliminary. The
following items, in priority order, close the gap to a full benchmark.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Higher-quality queries.} Replace the homogeneous template
    with queries that carry page-specific content. Two routes, in increasing
    cost: (a)~\emph{extractive / caption-based} pseudo-queries drawn from each
    page's own figure and table captions --- no LLM, deployable immediately,
    and a direct test of whether the ColPali/ColGemma3 zero scores survive
    non-homogeneous queries; (b)~\emph{LLM-authored} clinical questions via a
    hosted API or a self-hosted judge, with the \texttt{requires\_visual} and
    ambiguity filters of Section~\ref{sec:methodology-validation}. The
    Meditron-70B route additionally requires resolving the
    \texttt{torch}/\texttt{vllm} CUDA conflict on the cluster image.
  \item \textbf{Restore ColQwen3.} Pin a \texttt{colpali-engine} /
    \texttt{transformers} version compatible with the Qwen3-VL backbone and a
    resolvable checkpoint, then re-run the cell; the 320-d index path is
    already wired and configurable.
  \item \textbf{Scale the corpus.} Extend from the 709-page tiny palier to the
    1k/10k/50k paliers to observe the predicted retrieval-latency cliff at
    realistic index sizes. This requires fetching additional PMC-OA PDFs
    beyond the current 90.
  \item \textbf{Statistical robustness.} Add the two remaining seeds and run
    the planned bootstrap-CI, Holm-corrected pairwise tests.
  \item \textbf{Generation metrics and Track~B.} Once vLLM loads, add the
    RAGAS faithfulness/answer-relevancy generation metrics and execute the
    multilingual Track~B cells.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a system-integration release of a ColVision retrieval benchmark
in which the RAG pipeline is held constant and only the visual encoder
varies. On a 709-page figure-rich English medical corpus, four of five
encoders ran end-to-end through \textsc{mmore}'s process--index--retrieve
path. The robust outcome is a measured \emph{retrieval-latency--index-size
trade-off}: per-page vector counts differ $4.3\times$ across encoders and
drive an $8\times$ spread in per-query latency, with ColGemma3 the most
compact and ColPali the densest. Retrieval-quality numbers are reported as
preliminary, under template queries and a single seed, with two encoders at
zero pending higher-quality queries. The release establishes a reproducible,
provenance-tracked pipeline that makes the full multi-seed, multi-palier,
LLM-query run --- and Track~B --- straightforward to execute next.
